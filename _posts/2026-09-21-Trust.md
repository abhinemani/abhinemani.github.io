For years, the most reliable line in local government was some version of *we're not Washington*. Congress could melt down and the White House could dominate every news cycle, but the city still plowed the streets, and residents gave it credit for that. New data from the Partnership for Public Service's [State of Public Trust 2026](https://ourpublicservice.org/know-the-facts/resource-library/reports/state-of-trust-2026) report suggests that line is losing its power.

## The numbers, without the noise

The year-to-year swings in this survey are distracting, so ignore them. Compare 2022 to 2026 instead. Trust in local government fell from 54% to 41%, and trust in state government from 50% to 38%. Put plainly, about one in four people who trusted their city four years ago have stopped.

## The lockstep problem

The drop isn't even the most worrying part. It's that local trust now moves in step with federal trust. Last year, federal fell six points, local seven, state four. Cities used to be graded on their own curve. Now, when Washington's reputation sinks, city hall sinks with it, regardless of whether the buses ran on time.

Nearly half of Americans say their own experiences with government have been mostly positive. But only 37% think most *other* people's have been. We've collectively decided the DMV is miserable for everyone except ourselves. And experience matters enormously: people with good experiences are more than four times as likely to trust government as those with bad ones.

Two-thirds of Americans now call civil servants competent. Americans are perfectly able to distrust "government" while respecting the people who work in it. That distinction is an opening.

## Why a dashboard won't fix this

The obvious prescription is transparency: publish the metrics, show the work. I built LA's first Mayor's Performance Dashboard, and I've also written about the limits of dashboards since. A chart of permit turnaround times doesn't change how a resident feels. What changes it is the experience itself, and whether anyone hears about it.

The OECD research cited in the report backs this up: everyday service delivery drives trust in local government, while big-picture policy drives trust nationally. So the lever is in cities' hands, but it's not a data portal. It's closing the loop when someone reports a pothole. It's letting residents see the people who fixed it. It's making one good experience visible enough that the neighbors stop assuming theirs would be bad.

## One honest caveat

The 2026 survey had 1,000 respondents, a ±3.1 point margin, and was fielded during the Iran war and a partial shutdown, which likely pushed trust down across the board. Treat any single year with caution. The four-year slope, though, is hard to wish away.

"We're not Washington" was never really a strategy. It was a gift from the public, and they're taking it back. The cities that keep their residents' trust will be the ones that earn it one visible interaction at a time.
